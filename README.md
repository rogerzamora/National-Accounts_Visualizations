# National Accounts and GDP in Honduras - Research 

## Summary
This research from 2024 measures how much the cost of sending remittances takes away from Honduras' Gross Domestic Product (GDP). It was published by the Central America Journal, co-authored with Mauricio Elvir. Using quarterly data from 2013 to 2023, it forecasts GDP, remittance flows and remittance costs for every quarter of 2024 and 2025, and links them through a regression analysis.
Sending typical remittances of 200 and 500 US dollars from Spain and the United States costs Honduras 467 million US dollars in 2024 and 498 million in 2025. If these transfers were free, Honduras' GDP would be 1.28% higher in 2024 and 1.31% higher in 2025. All data processing, modelling, diagnostics and charts were built in R, and the full workflow is documented in a reproducible coding sample.

## Background
Remittances make up about 25% of Honduras' GDP, and almost 95% of them come from the United States (86.1%) and Spain (8.8%). Most studies of remittances focus on how much money arrives and what it means for families. Far fewer measure what is lost along the way. Every transfer carries fees, exchange-rate margins and interbank charges, and in each corridor millions of dollars are paid to transfer companies every year, which is very much related to IP trade commercialization networks.
The World Bank monitors these costs through its Remittance Prices Worldwide database, and the G20 has committed to reducing the global average to 3%. In 2024 the global average was 6.35%. What was missing for Honduras was a figure that could be read directly against the national accounts: how much these costs are worth in US dollars, and what share of GDP they represent. I designed this research to produce that figure and to project it forward.

## Data sources
The research combines five official sources into one quarterly dataset covering 2013 Q1 to 2023 Q4 (44 quarters). Remittance costs come from the World Bank's Remittance Prices Worldwide (RPW) database, which provides microdata on the total cost (%) of 200 and 500 USD transfers by firm, corridor and quarter. These were used to compute average costs for the Spain–Honduras and United States–Honduras corridors.
GDP comes from the Central Bank of Honduras' Quarterly National Accounts, at market prices, in current values and seasonally adjusted, in lempiras. It was converted into US dollars with the Central Bank's monthly series of the average lempira–US dollar exchange rate. Total remittance flows come from the Executive Secretariat of the Central American Monetary Council (SECMCA), which reports monthly family remittances to Honduras in millions of USD. Finally, the Central Bank's Semi-annual Family Remittance Survey (January 2024) gives the share of remittances by country of origin, which was used to split the flows by corridor.
The RPW measure is the total cost of each transfer, which already includes exchange-rate margins, interbank charges and transfer fees. GDP is measured at market prices in current values, the same basis used in the national accounts, so every result can be read directly as a share of GDP. The only assumption of the paper is that the corridor shares from the 2024 survey (8.8% Spain, 86.1% United States) apply to the whole period, so the corridor figures are estimates of these complex flows.

## Intended audience
The research is written for national authorities in Honduras, such as the Central Bank and government ministries, and for international organizations working on the G20 cost-reduction target. It is also aimed at banks and money transfer operators, and at academia. These readers need to know how much remittance costs take away from the economy, which corridors and firms are most expensive, and how this will change over time. The article presents its results in tables and charts that a non-technical reader can follow, while the coding sample lets researchers reproduce every step.

## Methodology
The work followed seven steps, all built in R. Each step produces an output the next one builds on, and the whole sequence can be rerun when new quarters of data are published.

- Data cleaning and corridor mapping. I imported the RPW microdata and selected and renamed the variables needed: period, source and destination country, firm, transfer speed, and the total cost of 200 and 500 USD transfers. Frequency tables identified the 10 firms with the most presence in each corridor, and grouped means (group_by and summarize) gave each firm's average cost. I then computed quarterly average costs for each corridor and amount from 2013 Q1 to 2023 Q4.
- Building the GDP series in US dollars. I took quarterly GDP at market prices in current values from the Central Bank of Honduras and converted it from lempiras to US dollars using the average exchange rate of each quarter. Monthly remittance flows from SECMCA were summed into quarters and split by corridor using the survey shares. The result is a set of eight quarterly variables: four cost series (%), GDP, and total, Spanish and US remittances (millions of USD). Each was converted into a time-series object (ts, starting 2013 Q1, frequency 4).
- Stationarity testing. ARIMA models require stationary series, so I applied the Augmented Dickey–Fuller test to every variable. In levels, none of the series was stationary; GDP, for example, returned a p-value of 0.95. I then took first differences (diff) and tested again to set the order of integration (d) of each model.
- Model selection. I fitted an ARIMA model to each series with forecast::auto.arima, which searches across the autoregressive (p), differencing (d) and moving-average (q) orders and selects the model with the best information criteria (AIC, AICc and BIC). GDP was best described by an ARIMA(0,1,0) with drift, meaning it grows by a steady average amount each quarter (about 99 million USD). The cost series needed richer structures, such as ARIMA(2,1,2) for 200 USD from Spain and ARIMA(3,1,0) for 500 USD from Spain.
- Residual diagnostics. I checked every model with checkresiduals and the Ljung–Box test. All p-values were above 0.05 (0.971 for GDP), so the residuals behave as white noise and the models capture the time dependence in the data. I also wrote two reusable ggplot2 functions that plot the density and the autocorrelation function (ACF) of each model's residuals with confidence bounds, and combined them into publication-ready panels with patchwork.
- Forecasting. Using the forecast function with a horizon of eight quarters (h = 8), I projected every variable for each quarter of 2024 and 2025, with 80% and 95% prediction intervals. GDP is forecast to rise from 8,940 million USD in 2024 Q1 to 9,636 million USD in 2025 Q4.
- Measuring the weight in GDP. Applying the forecast cost rates to the forecast remittance flows gives the US dollars lost in each quarter. Dividing by forecast GDP gives their weight in GDP, between 1.22% and 1.34% per quarter. Finally, a linear regression of GDP on the dollars lost to remittance costs (GDP = 2,940.34 + 26.04 × costs, R² = 0.91) describes how the two move together over time.
- This order puts econometric judgment where it matters most: testing, model choice and diagnostics. The repetitive parts, cleaning, transforming and plotting eight series, are automated with functions that can be reused for any other variable.

## Outputs
The research produced three concrete answers:
- Which firms create the largest economic drain on Honduras' national accounts? In the Spanish corridor, Western Union is the most expensive (almost 9% for 200 USD) and Santander Envíos the cheapest (about 3%). In the US corridor, Xoom is the most expensive (above 4.5% for 200 USD) and Viamericas the cheapest (2.5%).
- What is the weight of remittance costs in GDP? 467 million USD in 2024 and 498 million USD in 2025, equal to 1.28% and 1.31% of GDP.
- How will GDP, remittances and their costs evolve? Quarterly forecasts for 2024 and 2025, with 80% and 95% prediction intervals, show GDP rising from 8,940 million USD to 9,636 million USD.

## Relevance to the creative economy
Measuring the contribution of creative outputs to GDP raises the same questions this research answers. It means starting from firm-level or transaction-level data, converting it into values consistent with the national accounts, expressing it as a share of GDP and projecting it forward.
The parallel is direct. Creative industries generate cross-border flows, such as royalties, licensing fees and payments for digital content, which are recorded in the balance of payments much like remittances. Their value added sits inside GDP but is often not shown separately in official statistics. The methods used here transfer to that setting: harmonizing microdata in R, converting national-currency series into a common unit, testing, selecting and validating models, and estimating their weight in GDP. The same pipeline could estimate the quarterly contribution of copyright-based industries to GDP, forecast creative exports, or measure how much value is lost to transaction and platform costs in creative trade.

## Impact
The research shows that remittance costs are a measurable loss for the Honduran economy, not only for individual families. Its impact falls in five areas.
It puts a figure on what was missing: 467 million USD in 2024 and 498 million USD in 2025, close to 1 billion USD over two years, from typical transfers in two corridors alone.
It gives national and international authorities a number they can compare directly with GDP, supporting the G20 goal of reducing remittance costs to 3%.
It ranks corridors and firms by cost. Honduras is the fourth most expensive destination for 200 USD transfers from Spain, while the United States–Honduras corridor is among the cheapest for both amounts.
Every result can be traced to open data and R code, and the pipeline can be updated each quarter.
The method of measuring a flow, converting it to national-accounts terms and forecasting its weight in GDP applies to any sector, including the creative economy.

## Conclusion
Remittance costs do not come from the same data-generating process as the creative industries, whose outputs show stronger non-linearities. Even so, the research follows the same data procedure the CEDM could put in place once its indicators are gathered and built. The counterfactual used here, asking how much higher GDP would be if remittances were free, could be essential to price the contribution of the creative economy in each economy at the national level, before aggregating to the global level. In the same way, what falls outside the creative economy in each country's national accounts could be isolated using the CEDM indicators, verifying the share that creative firms account for in each economy.
